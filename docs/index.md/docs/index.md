# Tekken 3 Options and Difficulty: A Plain Reference

Most players never open Tekken 3's options screen, and the game plays perfectly well on its defaults. But a handful of settings quietly decide how hard the computer is, how long matches last and how much it costs to sit and block — and two minutes in that menu can fix the things that frustrate people about the game. This page explains the main options in the home version (the version carried by today's Android release, v1.3, about 61 MB), what each one actually changes, and the settings worth choosing for learning and for a real challenge.

## Difficulty

Difficulty controls how the computer plays against you in Arcade mode: how quickly it reacts, how hard it punishes mistakes, and how aggressive it becomes in the later stages. On the lower settings the early opponents are forgiving, which makes them good sparring partners while you learn a character's moves. On the higher settings the computer blocks more, steps around your attacks more, and takes bigger bites out of your health bar when you miss.

One thing difficulty does not control is unlocking. The extra characters are earned by finishing Arcade mode with different fighters, and that works on any difficulty — so there is no shame, and no penalty, in learning on an easier setting and raising it later.

## Rounds and round time

In Versus play you can set how many rounds win a match and how long each round lasts. Shorter rounds reward aggression: there is less time to recover from a bad start, and a life lead is easier to protect. Longer rounds favour patient players, because there is more time to work out what the other person is doing and come back.

If your matches keep ending on time with both players still standing, the setting to look at is round length, not difficulty.

## Guard Damage

Guard Damage decides whether blocking costs you health. With it on, every hit you block still chips away a small amount of damage, so a player who only defends will slowly lose. With it off, blocking is free, defence gets stronger, and matches tend to run longer.

Against the computer it is a difficulty lever in disguise — turning it on makes defensive opponents noticeably fairer to fight. Against a friend it is a matter of taste: on for faster, more decisive games; off for long, careful ones.

## Practice mode options

Practice is where Tekken 3's options matter most. You can set what the training dummy does — stand still, guard, or fight back — and bring the command list up on screen while you work through it.

The most useful habit: once you can do a move on a standing dummy, set the dummy to guard and try again. Half of looking good in this game is learning which of your favourite strings stop working the moment the other player simply holds back.

## Sound, vibration and the small stuff

The remaining options are comfort settings: music and sound levels, stereo or mono output, and controller vibration on the original hardware. None of them change the game itself. The one worth knowing is vibration — on a setup with rumble, turning it on gives you a physical jolt on big hits, which some players find genuinely helps their timing.

## Two setups worth using

**Learning the game:** lower difficulty, Guard Damage off, long rounds in Versus. You get time to think, mistakes are cheap, and you can study one character at a time.

**A real challenge:** the highest difficulty you can survive, Guard Damage on. The computer stops letting you hide, and every blocked string costs you something.

## Quick questions

**Q: Does difficulty affect which characters I unlock?**
A: No. Finishing Arcade mode with different fighters unlocks the hidden characters, on any difficulty setting.

**Q: Why do my matches keep ending on time?**
A: Usually the round timer, not your play. Lengthen the rounds in Versus, or turn Guard Damage on so blocking stops being a safe way to run the clock down.

**Q: Which setting should a beginner change first?**
A: Difficulty, one step down. It makes the early opponents patient enough to learn against, and you can raise it again whenever it starts feeling easy.
